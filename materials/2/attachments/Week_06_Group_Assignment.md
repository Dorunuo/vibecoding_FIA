## Learning objectives

By the end of this homework, you should be able to:

- **Analyze how an opening works:** explain how choices in image, sound, text or pacing shape what viewers understand and what they still want to know; adapt a technique without copying it.
- **Turn a complex story into a focused opening:** select the Titan Run facts viewers need, establish the stakes for a chosen audience, and arrange shots, sound and pacing around one unresolved question.
- **Research tools and AIGC video generation:** investigate accessible video generators and editors, weigh their capabilities, credit costs and limits, and choose an approach that fits your opening. Generating AIGC footage is optional.
- **Design and manage a production workflow:** turn your creative direction into a style frame and shot plan, then coordinate asset creation or sourcing, editing, checks and revision while managing limited credits and failed outputs.
- **Direct AI with human judgment:** use AI to explore opening and shot alternatives, then explain which ideas the group accepted, changed or rejected and why.
- **Improve the video through evidence:** identify what a first cut communicates well or poorly at specific moments, make a targeted change, and check the final export.
- **Account for the finished work:** explain asset rights and AI use, identify each member’s real contribution, and reflect individually on what the process taught you.

### Video synopsis — The Titan Run

For generations, Titan Habitat has been home to a human settlement beneath the light of a brilliant blue-white star.

Inside its shielded walls are gardens, workshops, schools and homes. Outside, ships travel between mining outposts, industrial platforms and the Titan Gate—the settlement’s connection to the wider human world.

Every vessel has a silicon mind—an intelligence far beyond that of any human, yet aware of its own limits. For decades, humans have entrusted these minds with full authority over vessel operations, including navigation and emergency decisions. People aboard are inhabitants or passengers, not vessel operators; they cannot take the helm or override a mind's decision. Some minds carry passengers for a living. Others spend their days refining fuel, tending crops, repairing machinery or surveying empty space.

Then, on an ordinary working day, a heavy cargo freighter approaches Titan Habitat with a shipment of industrial propellant.

During its final docking maneuver, it loses control.

Warnings race across the docking network. Docking arms release. Nearby vessels try to pull clear.

The freighter strikes the habitat.

Its cargo tanks rupture, and secondary explosions tear through the connected docking bays. Some ships are crushed against their berths. Others lose engines, power or access to open space. Emergency doors contain part of the destruction, but the surviving inhabited sections are losing air.

An evacuation call goes out.

**All seventeen ships still capable of flight answer.**

Some are already working nearby. Others disconnect from surviving docks. They open passenger decks, medical wards, cargo compartments and maintenance spaces. Survivors from the habitat and the stranded vessels board whichever ship can take them.

Atlas takes hundreds. Peregrine, a small courier, takes twelve. Loomfield clears space among its growing beds. Workboats secure emergency passenger cassettes in spaces never intended for people.

When the last loading hatch closes, the seventeen ships carry **1,344 evacuees**.

Every ship leaves full.

Behind them, Titan Habitat burns against the calm blue-white light of its star. Ahead, the rescue mothership *Asteria* broadcasts from beyond the Titan Gate.

Asteria has enough berths for everyone. But it cannot dock with or refuel the seventeen vessels. Its rescue skiffs must collect their passengers at the gate and bring them across. By the time the fleet can arrive, dwindling life-support reserves will allow only three final rounds of transfers.

First, the ships must reach it.

Collision debris crosses the approach lanes. Damaged navigation beacons give incomplete readings. Some routes are clear but long; others offer a shortcut through uncertain wreckage. Several ships escaped with damage. Others have too little fuel or life support for an extended journey.

Each mind knows its own ship intimately. None sees the whole situation.

Nova can scout ahead. Carillon can authenticate signals and measure beacon timing. Peregrine can relay a critical message when ordinary communications fail. Orion carries transferable propellant. Forge can manufacture a few additional fuel packets. Suture can repair damaged equipment. Other ships contribute medicine, oxygen, protective panels and towing power.

Every act of help consumes something finite.

At the gate, the fleet faces one last constraint: Asteria’s normal transfer rate will leave **39 people behind**.

Qilin, a gate inspection and alignment craft, has an emitter that can briefly stabilize an additional transfer lane. One correctly timed pulse could create room for fifty more people in a rescue round.

But Qilin cannot do it alone. It needs independent measurements from Nova and Carillon, physical assistance to preserve enough fuel for the emitter, and a transfer schedule that protects its own passengers while making room for others.

The seventeen minds must negotiate that schedule themselves.

Each follows the same ordered duties: protect the humans aboard its own ship first, protect other humans second, and preserve its original running mind third.

A mind must decide what evidence to trust, which promises to accept and how much help it can safely offer. A delayed reply may mean damaged communications. An unexpected approach may be an offer of assistance. Under pressure, either can be mistaken for a threat.

Cooperation is possible. So are refusal, misunderstanding and conflict.

As air reserves fall and the rescue windows approach, every commitment changes another ship’s chances. Fuel promised too late cannot power an engine. A repair offered too late cannot open a passenger hatch. A warning without evidence may send a ship toward danger—or make it abandon its only viable route.

Seventeen ships escaped the same disaster.

Whether all their passengers survive now depends on what their minds choose to do for one another.

**Bring all 1,344 people to Asteria before time runs out.**

### Making your opening

Use this synopsis as the shared story backdrop. Choose an opening moment—a warning, collision, departure, message or choice—and leave the outcome unresolved. You do not need to cover the whole synopsis, show every named ship or explain the game mechanics in 30–120 seconds. You may invent people, dialogue, sounds and visual details that fit the backdrop; identify your additions in the report. No official route, winner or ending is supplied, and you do not need the instructor’s draft video.

**AI ALLOWED — find and watch an opening example.** Before planning your video, find and watch one opening sequence from a movie or game through a source you can access lawfully without buying anything; an instructor-provided clip also works. An opening sequence appears at the start, establishes a world, tone, question or stakes, and invites viewers into the work. It is not a trailer or a recap of the whole plot. In the group report, name the movie or game, the opening you watched, its source or link, and the date. If you cannot access an example, ask the instructor/TA for one; do not claim a viewing you did not complete.

**NO AI — observe and adapt.** In your own words, identify two concrete choices in that opening (such as image, sound, text or pacing) and what each helps viewers understand or wonder. Choose one technique to adapt for *Titan Run* without copying footage, characters or music.

**AI REQUIRED — explore with your own harness or AILab.** Treat AI as your co-director. Brainstorm to generate versions of opening ideas, possible shot order and what viewers should understand. In the group report, save the exact main prompts and final AI shot-planning outputs you used, and identify the harness or interface and displayed model/mode. You can iterate to improve the results.

**NO AI — choose the direction.** Together as a team, decide the audience, one-sentence premise, tone, unresolved final question and essential facts. Mark the details you invented. Write this record yourselves.

**AI ALLOWED — make the style frame and video.** Choose or make the authorized image, plan at least four purposeful shots and assemble the video. You may use AI or edit manually. To save generation credits, you may draft short clips at 480p when your tool offers a lower-cost setting, then upscale only the clips you keep. Check the tool's actual credit cost first. Upscaling can improve size and apparent sharpness, but it will not reliably fix a wrong action, distorted detail or unreadable text; regenerate a selected shot at higher quality if needed. The submitted video must still meet the final format and resolution above. Check continuity, text, sound and pacing; unedited generated clips alone are not a finished video. Record each outside asset's source and use rights, and the tool/model or mode for generated assets. Do not copy a named creator or franchise, or use a real person's face or voice without permission. No paid account or editable project file is required.

**NO AI — inspect and decide.** Watch the full first cut. Note one timestamped detail that works and one that does not. Choose one change yourselves. Revise (**AI ALLOWED**), export the MP4 and watch it again. Record the change and what you noticed. You need not submit the first cut.

**NO AI — contributions and final check.** Record each member's actual work. Each member watches the final video and confirms the asset rights and their own contribution record.

- Video generation can use a large number of tokens or credits. One long or repeated prompt may exhaust your weekly allowance. Plan short shots and check the cost shown by your chosen tool before each run. The required 30–120-second finished video can also use editing, still images, sound and text.

Jimeng advertises daily free credits. Check the current free allowance, available model and any student offer in your own account before relying on them or paying. A subscription is optional, but may greatly expedite your work. 
- Many video tools are available. You may research accessible options and choose the tool or combination that best fits your shots and budget. No particular video-generation tool is required.
